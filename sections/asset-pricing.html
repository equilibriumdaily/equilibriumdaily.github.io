    Rough Heston Variance at Zero: Negative Moments, Conditional Paths and a Test for the Zero Event

    In the rough Heston model the instantaneous variance is exactly zero with positive probability at every date. Without drift, the variance also puts so much probability just above zero that the expected inverse variance is infinite even after the zero atom is excluded. One root of a Gamma-function equation sets both how much probability lies near zero and how fast the expected variance falls into a zero.

    No-Arbitrage Does Not Imply an Equivalent Calibrated Pricing Measure

    When a stock trades over two dates and options on its final price are priced by the market, one usually expects that ruling out arbitrage guarantees a pricing measure that agrees with the physical probabilities on which events are possible, prices every option correctly and makes the stock a fair game. In a simple, well-behaved market this fails: there is no arbitrage, yet every pricing measure that fits the option prices ignores a state that has physical probability one half.

    Does Stamp Duty Make UK Shares Cheap? Prices Say No, Takeovers Say Maybe

    The UK charges a 0.5% tax every time a UK share is bought, and a common argument holds that this makes UK shares cheap and UK companies easy takeover targets. If buyers priced in every future payment of the tax, the discount would be about 3% for the median stock and larger for stocks that trade often. In the two settings where only the tax differs, prices show no such discount; the only support comes from takeover bids, and it may reflect UK takeover rules rather than the tax.

    The Pre-FOMC Drift Lost Its Morning

    Stocks have earned a well-known excess return in the hours before Federal Reserve announcements. Studies using daily closing prices conclude that this drift has faded, but it has moved rather than disappeared: since 2011 it is earned overnight before the announcement day, not during the morning of that day.
