  • No. 00098 November 2026Macroeconomics
    Growth in Endogenous Production Networks Needs Compatible Recipes: A Correction to Acemoglu and Azar (2020)

    Acemoglu and Azar (2020) show that when firms choose their suppliers, the growth rate equals the rate at which the best technologies improve, multiplied by the full input-output network. That product assumes firms can use the best technology and keep all their important suppliers at the same time. When the best new technologies require dropping an important supplier, firms must give up either productivity or network amplification, and growth falls short of the formula.

  • No. 00088 November 2026Information Economics
    Wisdom of Crowds with Many Separate Groups: A Correction to Golub and Jackson (2010)

    Golub and Jackson (2010) showed that a society that averages its neighbours' opinions learns the truth exactly when no one keeps a large influence. That equivalence breaks when the society splits into many separate groups: each group may learn well, yet with so many groups at least one of them is almost sure to end far from the truth. The note gives a counterexample, a corrected statement, and a sharp rule for equal-sized groups: their size must grow faster than the logarithm of their number.

  • No. 00078 November 2026Consumer Economics
    Engel's Law Holds for Groceries, Not for Restaurants

    Poorer households spend a larger share of their budget on food, and this still holds in US data up to 2024. The pattern comes entirely from groceries: spending on restaurants rises in proportion to the budget in every income group.

  • No. 00068 November 2026Sports Economics
    The Pythagorean Expectation, 45 Seasons Later

    Bill James's formula linking a baseball team's wins to its runs scored and allowed has worked unchanged in the 45 seasons since it was published. Records that beat the formula are mostly luck and do not carry over, so a team's quality is better read from its runs than from its wins.

  • No. 00058 November 2026Game Theory
    Who Bargains Next? Recognition and Cooperative Allocations

    Who proposes after an offer is rejected looks like a procedural detail, but it changes the outcome of coalitional bargaining. If the player who rejects proposes next, patient players no longer reach the Shapley value: every player's deviation from an equal share is amplified.

  • No. 00048 November 2026Urban Economics
    Zipf's Law for US Cities, 2000-2024

    Zipf's law, under which a city's size is inversely proportional to its rank, is often treated as a constant of urban economics. In US data for 2000-2024 it holds only for metropolitan areas at particular cut-offs; incorporated cities follow a clean power law, but with an exponent clearly above 1.

  • No. 00038 November 2026Asset Pricing
    The Pre-FOMC Drift Lost Its Morning

    Stocks have earned a well-known excess return in the hours before Federal Reserve announcements. Studies using daily closing prices conclude that this drift has faded, but it has moved rather than disappeared: since 2011 it is earned overnight before the announcement day, not during the morning of that day.

  • No. 00028 November 2026Game Theory
    The Information Complexity of Nash Equilibrium

    How many noisy observations of payoffs are needed to find an approximate Nash equilibrium of a small zero-sum game with high confidence? The answer depends only on the equilibrium mixing probabilities, is largest when equilibrium play is uniform, and can be far smaller than estimating every payoff first and then solving the game.

  • No. 00018 November 2026Mechanism Design
    The Price of a Place in Line

    A regulator must decide whose infrastructure project goes first but cannot verify how valuable or reliable a developer's project is. Collateral is then the only tool that makes developers report truthfully. The note computes the exact deposit needed for efficient priority, shows that it exceeds what either pivotal developer gains from priority, and shows that a priority lottery is optimal when collateral is scarcer.
