Why Equilibrium Daily exists

Academic publishing claims to select research on its merits. The evidence below shows that who you are and whom you know weigh heavily on whether a paper is published. We publish every result in the open, with its full proof, so that it can be judged on its content alone.

Our experience

We have seen editors reject papers written by less known authors and then accept the same work once prominent scholars were added as co-authors. The findings did not change; the names on the title page did. In our experience, success depends on marketing, networking and pitching far more than on the results. This is not an isolated impression. Controlled experiments and large data sets on journals document the same pattern.

2. Connections to editors raise publication

  • Colussi (2018), Review of Economics and Statistics. In the American Economic Review, Econometrica, the Journal of Political Economy and the Quarterly Journal of Economics (2000-2006), about 43% of articles were written by scholars connected to an editor at the time of publication. When an editor takes charge, his faculty colleagues and former PhD students publish more in that journal: about two more papers over three years.
  • Brogaard, Engelberg and Parsons (2014), Journal of Financial Economics. In more than 50,000 articles from 30 economics and finance journals, an editor's current university colleagues publish about 100% more papers in the editor's journal during the editor's tenure than outside it.
  • Ductor and Visser (2023), Journal of Economic Surveys. Across 106 economics journals and 6,192 board members (1990-2011), the authors describe economics as a discipline with a high concentration of institutional and individual power over editorial boards, especially at the most prestigious journals.

3. Review outcomes are close to a lottery

  • NeurIPS consistency experiments (2014 and 2021). The same papers were reviewed by two independent committees. Of the papers accepted by one committee, 49.5% (2014) and 50.6% (2021) were rejected by the other. With an acceptance rate of 22.7%, a purely random process would give about 77%; review is better than a coin, but half of all acceptances depend on which committee a paper happens to receive.
  • Cole, Cole and Simon (1981), Science. 150 National Science Foundation proposals were re-evaluated by independent reviewers. Whether a proposal is funded depends substantially on chance: in many cases the identity of the assigned reviewers determines the outcome.

4. Standards are not the same for everyone

  • Hengel (2022), Economic Journal. Female-authored papers in top economics journals are 1% to 6% better written than comparable papers by men, the gap widens during peer review, and their papers spend longer under review. The evidence is consistent with women being held to higher standards.

5. Careers depend on a handful of gatekeepers

  • Heckman and Moktan (2020), Journal of Economic Literature. Publications in the five top economics journals have a powerful influence on tenure decisions in the top 35 US economics departments, even though many influential papers are published elsewhere. The authors argue that this screen rewards careerism over creativity and gives a small number of editors control over careers.
  • Merton (1968), Science. The "Matthew effect": eminent scientists receive disproportionate credit for work of comparable quality, while lesser known researchers receive less.

What the defenders of the system point to

Several of the same studies find that papers by connected or prolific authors are cited more after publication (Laband and Piette 1994; Brogaard, Engelberg and Parsons 2014; Card and DellaVigna 2020). Their authors read this as evidence that editors use information about authors to select better papers. We think this argument is circular: citations themselves depend on the prominence of authors and journals, so a system that favours established names will also produce more citations for them. Either way, a reader has no means of checking the decision; the reasons stay inside the editorial process.

What we do instead

We do not believe this system can be fixed by small adjustments. It should be replaced by open publication in which results are judged on their content. Equilibrium Daily applies this principle:

  • Every result is published with its complete proof, free to read and free to use.
  • Nothing depends on the name, affiliation or network of the author.
  • Anyone can check a proof and point out an error in public.
