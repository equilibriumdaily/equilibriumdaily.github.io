    Noise Beats Coarseness: The Value of Inside Information in the Kyle-Back Model

    Two signals that explain the same share of a normal fundamental's variance can be worth very different amounts to an insider in the continuous-time Kyle model. The sign of the fundamental explains 63.7 percent of its variance and earns 63.7 percent of the full-information profit, while normal noisy information of the same precision earns 79.8 percent. The insider must steer order flow to a normally distributed endpoint to stay hidden, so information whose shape is far from normal is worth less.

  • No. 001612 November 2026Macroeconomics
    The Feldstein-Horioka Puzzle Is Back

    Feldstein and Horioka (1980) found that countries that save more also invest more, almost one for one, as if capital could not cross borders. By the mid-2000s the link had nearly vanished. In World Bank data up to 2024 it has returned: across OECD countries, about half of a country's extra saving again shows up as extra investment at home, as in the 1980s, because current-account imbalances have shrunk.

    Rough Heston Variance at Zero: Negative Moments, Conditional Paths and a Test for the Zero Event

    In the rough Heston model the instantaneous variance is exactly zero with positive probability at every date. Without drift, the variance also puts so much probability just above zero that the expected inverse variance is infinite even after the zero atom is excluded. One root of a Gamma-function equation sets both how much probability lies near zero and how fast the expected variance falls into a zero.

    No-Arbitrage Does Not Imply an Equivalent Calibrated Pricing Measure

    When a stock trades over two dates and options on its final price are priced by the market, one usually expects that ruling out arbitrage guarantees a pricing measure that agrees with the physical probabilities on which events are possible, prices every option correctly and makes the stock a fair game. In a simple, well-behaved market this fails: there is no arbitrage, yet every pricing measure that fits the option prices ignores a state that has physical probability one half.

  • No. 001311 November 2026Urban Economics
    Urban Growth Replicates, With Three Fixes

    Duranton and Puga (2023) estimate that relaxing planning limits in seven expensive US cities would raise national output by about 8%. An independent rewrite of their public code reproduces every headline number exactly, and that figure survives. Three coding errors change smaller numbers: freezing cities at their 1950 sizes costs about a fifth of consumption rather than a quarter, and Los Angeles enters the policy by only 22 dollars.

  • No. 001210 November 2026Information Economics
    Five Small Errors in Bayesian Persuasion

    Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) founded information design, and a line-by-line check confirms its main theorems: with finitely many states, the sender's best signal is found by concavifying his payoff, an optimum exists, and it needs no more signal realizations than there are states. Five statements in the fine print are wrong as written, including a popular rule of thumb that two concave functions mean persuasion cannot help, and the web appendix's formula for infinitely many states. Each has a one-line fix.

  • No. 00119 November 2026Asset Pricing
    Does Stamp Duty Make UK Shares Cheap? Prices Say No, Takeovers Say Maybe

    The UK charges a 0.5% tax every time a UK share is bought, and a common argument holds that this makes UK shares cheap and UK companies easy takeover targets. If buyers priced in every future payment of the tax, the discount would be about 3% for the median stock and larger for stocks that trade often. In the two settings where only the tax differs, prices show no such discount; the only support comes from takeover bids, and it may reflect UK takeover rules rather than the tax.

  • No. 00109 November 2026Industrial Organization
    Do US Firm Sizes Follow Zipf's Law? In the Middle, Yes. At the Top, No

    Axtell (2001) found that US firm sizes follow Zipf's law: a firm ten times larger is ten times rarer, from the smallest firms to the largest. In public data up to 2023 this holds remarkably well between about 5 and 10,000 employees, but not at the top: Zipf predicts a largest US firm of about 12 million employees, while the largest actual employer has about 2 million worldwide. The giants are too few and too small, which matters because Zipf is the case in which shocks to the largest firms move the whole economy.

  • No. 00098 November 2026Macroeconomics
    Growth in Endogenous Production Networks Needs Compatible Recipes: A Correction to Acemoglu and Azar (2020)

    Acemoglu and Azar (2020) show that when firms choose their suppliers, the growth rate equals the rate at which the best technologies improve, multiplied by the full input-output network. That product assumes firms can use the best technology and keep all their important suppliers at the same time. When the best new technologies require dropping an important supplier, firms must give up either productivity or network amplification, and growth falls short of the formula.

  • No. 00088 November 2026Information Economics
    Wisdom of Crowds with Many Separate Groups: A Correction to Golub and Jackson (2010)

    Golub and Jackson (2010) showed that a society that averages its neighbors' opinions learns the truth exactly when no one keeps a large influence. That equivalence breaks when the society splits into many separate groups: each group may learn well, yet with so many groups at least one of them is almost sure to end far from the truth. The note gives a counterexample, a corrected statement, and a sharp rule for equal-sized groups: their size must grow faster than the logarithm of their number.

    The Pythagorean Expectation, 45 Seasons Later

    Bill James's formula linking a baseball team's wins to its runs scored and allowed has worked unchanged in the 45 seasons since it was published. Records that beat the formula are mostly luck and do not carry over, so a team's quality is better read from its runs than from its wins.

    The Price of a Place in Line
