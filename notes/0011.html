Does Stamp Duty Make UK Shares Cheap? Prices Say No, Takeovers Say Maybe

The UK charges a 0.5% tax every time a UK share is bought, and a common argument holds that this makes UK shares cheap and UK companies easy takeover targets. If buyers priced in every future payment of the tax, the discount would be about 3% for the median stock and larger for stocks that trade often. In the two settings where only the tax differs, prices show no such discount; the only support comes from takeover bids, and it may reflect UK takeover rules rather than the tax.

Result

If buyers capitalize all future stamp duty, a UK share's price discount is about 0.005 T/r0.005\,T/r0.005T/r, where TTT is annual turnover and rrr the discount rate. Across 15 European markets, UK bid probability rises with turnover more steeply than in untaxed markets, but a within-UK test cannot rule out no effect.

Summary

The UK's 0.5% stamp duty on share purchases is often blamed for cheap UK valuations, foreign investors' retreat and takeovers of UK companies; if buyers capitalize every future payment, the price discount is about 0.005 T/r0.005\,T/r0.005T/r, around 3% for the median UK stock with r=8%r = 8\%r=8%. When AIM shares became exempt in 2013-14, AIM stocks beat still-taxed small Main Market firms by only 0.49% on the announcement day, an unremarkable spread, and frequently traded stocks gained no more than rarely traded ones, although the tax predicts up to a 5.5% gain; AIM turnover rose by about 3.5%, but foreign institutional ownership did not change measurably. Companies with two share lines show no tax discount either: Unilever's London line traded at parity with its Dutch twin for 14 years, Carnival's London line traded at a premium for most of 2004-16, and RELX's London line traded 3.8% above its Dutch line in 2015-18. Across 109,000 firm-years in 15 European markets, UK companies whose shares trade more are more likely to receive a bid, more steeply than in untaxed markets, but this cross-country pattern may reflect UK takeover rules, and the within-UK test after the AIM exemption is too imprecise to confirm it. The estimates are presented as first-pass and differ from earlier evidence that UK prices rose when stamp duty was cut from 2% in the 1980s.
