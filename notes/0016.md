# The Feldstein-Horioka Puzzle Is Back

Equilibrium Daily, No. 0016, 12 November 2026. Section: Macroeconomics.

- Page: https://equilibriumdaily.github.io/notes/0016.html
- Full note with proof (PDF): https://equilibriumdaily.github.io/pdf/0016.pdf
- Drafted by an AI model and reviewed by a person before publication.

## Intuition

Feldstein and Horioka (1980) found that countries that save more also invest more, almost one for one, as if capital could not cross borders. By the mid-2000s the link had nearly vanished. In World Bank data up to 2024 it has returned: across OECD countries, about half of a country's extra saving again shows up as extra investment at home, as in the 1980s, because current-account imbalances have shrunk.

## Result

Across the 38 OECD countries, the slope of the period-average investment rate on the period-average national saving rate fell from 0.56 in 1980-89 and 0.65 in 1990-99 to 0.16 (s.e. 0.11) in 2000-07 and has returned to 0.51 (s.e. 0.06) in 2016-24; across all countries with population above one million it rose from 0.00 to 0.54 (s.e. 0.06).

## Summary

If capital moved freely, a country's investment would not depend on its own saving, yet Feldstein and Horioka (1980) found a cross-country slope of investment on saving close to 0.9 for 16 OECD countries in 1960-74. Using the World Development Indicators for 1980-2024, the note runs the same regression for successive periods, one observation per country, with robust standard errors. Because the current account equals saving minus investment, the slope equals $1 - \mathrm{cov}(CA,S)/\mathrm{var}(S)$ and falls when high savers run large surpluses. The OECD slope fell to 0.16 in 2000-07 and is 0.51 in 2016-24, with 2008-15 in between at 0.43; the rebound survives a balanced panel, dropping financial centers and oil exporters, using domestic instead of national saving, and counting only fixed investment, and in the euro area excluding Ireland and Luxembourg the slope moved from about zero to 0.41. The mechanism is in the current accounts: their cross-country standard deviation rose from 2.5% of GDP in the 1980s to 5.9% in 2000-07 and is now 3.9%, while the spread of saving rates changed little. The slope summarizes how far investment tracks saving and is not a clean measure of capital mobility, but it implies that about half of a gap in national saving now shows up as a gap in domestic investment.
