# Engel's Law Holds for Groceries, Not for Restaurants

Equilibrium Daily, No. 0007, 8 November 2026. Section: Consumer Economics.

- Page: https://equilibriumdaily.github.io/notes/0007.html
- Full note with proof (PDF): https://equilibriumdaily.github.io/pdf/0007.pdf
- Drafted by an AI model and reviewed by a person before publication.

## Intuition

Poorer households spend a larger share of their budget on food, and this still holds in US data up to 2024. The pattern comes entirely from groceries: spending on restaurants rises in proportion to the budget in every income group.

## Result

In the US Consumer Expenditure Survey, Engel's law holds in every year from 1984 to 2024: across the five income groups the elasticity of food spending with respect to total spending averages 0.77 and is below one in all 41 years. The law is driven entirely by groceries (elasticity 0.58), while spending on food away from home has an elasticity of 1.07, above one in 40 of 41 years.

## Summary

Engel (1857) observed that poorer families spend a larger share of their budget on food, so that food has an income elasticity below one. Using published BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey means for each income fifth, 1984-2024, the note estimates each year's elasticity as the slope of log spending on log total expenditures across the five groups, separately for all food, groceries and eating out. In 2024 groceries fell from 11.0% of spending in the lowest fifth to 6.2% in the highest, while eating out took between 4.7% and 5.2% in every group; for the average household the food share fell from 14.7% in 1984-1993 to 12.8% in 2005-2019 and has not fallen since. The official US poverty line still rests on Orshansky's multiplier of three times a minimal food budget, whereas 2024 food shares of 15.7% in the lowest fifth and 12.9% on average would imply a multiplier of about 6 to 8. Because eating out rises at least in proportion to the budget, methods that use Engel curves to estimate CPI bias should rely on groceries rather than on all food.
