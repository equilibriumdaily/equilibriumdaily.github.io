The Pythagorean Expectation, 45 Seasons Later

Bill James's formula linking a baseball team's wins to its runs scored and allowed has worked unchanged in the 45 seasons since it was published. Records that beat the formula are mostly luck and do not carry over, so a team's quality is better read from its runs than from its wins.

Result

Bill James's Pythagorean expectation W/L=(RS/RA)kW/L = (RS/RA)^kW/L=(RS/RA)k survives a 45-season out-of-sample test: the exponent is k=1.847k = 1.847k=1.847 on 1901-1980 and k=1.848k = 1.848k=1.848 on 1981-2025, the 1901-1980 fit predicts later wins with an error of about 4 per 162 games and explains 88% of their variation, and wins above or below the formula do not persist: about 92% disappears the next season.

Summary

The Pythagorean expectation, published around 1980, relates a baseball team's ratio of wins to losses to its ratio of runs scored to runs allowed, and every season after 1980 provides a clean holdout sample. Using 2,706 Major League team-seasons from the Lahman Baseball Database, the note estimates kkk as the slope of log⁡(W/L)\log(W/L)log(W/L) on log⁡(RS/RA)\log(RS/RA)log(RS/RA) before and after publication and finds the same value to the third decimal, with out-of-sample root mean squared error of 4.06 wins against 4.16 for James's original k=2k = 2k=2. The exponent varies by decade between 1.74 and 1.98 but has no trend, and letting it rise with runs per game does not improve the out-of-sample error. Wins above the formula (luck, with a standard deviation of 4.2 wins) have a next-season correlation of only 0.04 before 1980 and 0.08 after, and in forecasting next season's record the predicted share receives about three times the weight of the actual share. The same task, separating a persistent component from a large transitory one, arises when judging fund managers, executives or forecasters from short records.
