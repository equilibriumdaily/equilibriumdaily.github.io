When Interest Rates Ignored Inflation

Under the gold standard, British long-term interest rates moved with the level of prices rather than with inflation, the regularity Keynes called Gibson's paradox. In two and a half centuries of Bank of England data the paradox appears whenever the pound was tied to gold and disappears under paper money, both during the Napoleonic suspension of convertibility and since 1973, when yields follow inflation instead. This is what Barsky and Summers (1988) predict if a fixed gold price makes the price level move with gold's real value.

Result

Over 1822-1913 the British consol yield has a correlation of 0.45 with the log wholesale price level around a linear trend, 0.47 around a cubic trend and 0.61 when both series are measured against their own 31-year moving averages, and 0.06 with inflation over the previous five years. During the Restriction of 1797-1821 the correlation with the detrended price level is −0.14-0.14−0.14 and with inflation 0.41, and over 1973-2016 the correlation with inflation is 0.85. Across the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor countries the median correlation of long-term yields with inflation is 0.03 under the gold standard and 0.83 after 1973, positive in all 18.

Summary

Fisher's theory says nominal interest rates should move with expected inflation, the rate of change of prices, yet Gibson found British yields moving with the price level itself. The note measures the correlation of the consol yield with the detrended log wholesale price level and with five-year inflation in each British monetary regime since 1729, using the Bank of England's millennium dataset. Under gold the price-level relation holds across several long price swings, and a high price level did not forecast inflation over the next decade, so expected inflation does not explain it. Under inconvertible paper money the price-level relation turns negative and yields follow inflation, both during the Restriction, which coincided with the French wars, and since 1973. In the Jorda-Schularick-Taylor data the gold-standard correlation with the price level is positive in 16 of 17 countries around a linear trend, but the 1875-1913 window holds essentially one shared price swing, which a quadratic trend removes; the contrast that survives every measure is the correlation with inflation. The correlations describe comovement and do not separate the Barsky-Summers channel from other regime-dependent explanations.

    The Cost Disease Costs a Tenth of a Point

    Baumol's cost disease says that services with slow productivity growth, such as health care and education, become relatively more expensive, and take a growing share of spending if demand for them is price-inelastic.     The Hotelling Rule Holds Only on Average

    Hotelling's rule says that the scarcity rent on an exhaustible resource should grow at the rate of interest. Over 65 years, the real prices of oil, metals and other exhaustible resources have risen relative to farm commodities by about what the rule implies at the average real interest rate. Over shorter periods the relation runs the wrong way: exhaustible prices fell fastest when real rates were highest, in the 1980s, and rose fastest when rates were low, in the 2000s.

    Burgernomics After 25 Years: The Gap Closes Through Burger Prices

    The Economist's Big Mac index calls a currency overvalued when a burger costs more there in dollars than in the United States, which suggests the currency should fall. Over 25 years of the index, overvalued currencies have been no more likely to depreciate than any other. The burger gaps that do close, close because local menu prices catch up with past currency moves. National price levels under floating rates adjust through the exchange rate instead; only under the classical gold standard did price levels do the closing, as Big Mac prices do today.
