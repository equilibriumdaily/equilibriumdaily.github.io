# Wisdom of Crowds with Many Separate Groups: A Correction to Golub and Jackson (2010)

Equilibrium Daily, No. 0008, 8 November 2026. Section: Information Economics.

- Full note with proof (PDF): https://equilibriumdaily.github.io/pdf/0008.pdf

## Intuition

Golub and Jackson (2010) showed that a society that averages its neighbors' opinions learns the truth exactly when no one keeps a large influence. That equivalence breaks when the society splits into many separate groups: each group may learn well, yet with so many groups at least one of them is almost sure to end far from the truth. The note gives a counterexample, a corrected statement, and a sharp rule for equal-sized groups: their size must grow faster than the logarithm of their number.

## Result

For networks with a growing number of separate closed groups, vanishing maximal influence $s_1(n) \to 0$ does not imply wisdom: a society of $4^m$ groups of $m$ agents satisfies every assumption of Golub and Jackson (2010) with $s_1(n) = 1/m$, yet with probability $1-(1-2^{-m})^{4^m} \to 1$ some agent ends at belief one when the truth is one half. In general, the society is wise if and only if $\sum_{c=1}^{K_n} \Pr(|Y_c - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, where $Y_c$ is the consensus of closed group $c$; the original equivalence holds when the number of groups $K_n$ is bounded, and for $K_n$ equally weighted groups of size $m_n$ with fair-coin signals, the society is wise if and only if $m_n \to \infty$ and $(\log K_n)/m_n \to 0$.

## Summary

Under naive DeGroot learning, Proposition 2 of Golub and Jackson (2010) states that a sequence of convergent networks is wise, meaning all agents are simultaneously close to the truth, if and only if the largest influence vanishes, and the paper states that its results apply to all convergent networks. The proof shows that each agent learns individually, but wisdom requires all agents to be right at the same time, and with many independent closed groups the two requirements differ: in a network of $4^m$ groups of $m$ agents with fair-coin signals, the largest influence is $1/m$ and Theorem 4's balance and minimal in-dispersion conditions hold, yet the probability that some group is unanimously wrong tends to one. The corrected proposition characterizes wisdom by the sum over groups of each group's probability of error, keeps the 'only if' direction, restores the original equivalence when the number of closed groups is bounded (including every strongly connected network), and gives a distribution-free sufficient condition, $\max_c Q_c (1 + \log K_n) \to 0$. For equally weighted groups this condition is sharp: using Hoeffding's inequality and a method-of-types bound, the society is wise exactly when group size grows faster than the logarithm of the number of groups. Vanishing maximal influence still makes the society accurate on average, Theorem 1 is not exposed because its out-dispersion condition bounds the number of closed groups, and the paper's central message, that a society learns well only if no agent's influence stays large, is unaffected.
