Five Small Errors in Bayesian Persuasion

Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) founded information design, and a line-by-line check confirms its main theorems: with finitely many states, the sender's best signal is found by concavifying his payoff, an optimum exists, and it needs no more signal realizations than there are states. Five statements in the fine print are wrong as written, including a popular rule of thumb that two concave functions mean persuasion cannot help, and the web appendix's formula for infinitely many states. Each has a one-line fix.

Result

The main theorems of Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) hold, but five statements do not: (1) with infinitely many states the value must be the closed convex hull, the smallest upper semicontinuous concave function above v^\hat vv^, since in an example meeting all the web appendix's assumptions its formula gives the sender 0 while full revelation gives 1; (2) a concave receiver action and a concave sender payoff do not rule out gains from persuasion, as with action p\sqrt{p}p​ and sender payoff −a-a−a; (3) in the lobbying application with α<1/2\alpha < 1/2α<1/2, no disclosure is optimal but not uniquely; (4) a worked example ignores the constraint a≥0a \ge 0a≥0, so its bound is (1+E[ω])/2(1 + E[\omega])/2(1+E[ω])/2 rather than 1; (5) Proposition 2's genericity claim needs every action to be uniquely optimal at some belief.

Summary

Bayesian persuasion characterizes the sender's optimal value as the concave closure of his payoff as a function of the receiver's belief; with finitely many states this is correct, an optimum exists and at most as many signal realizations as states are needed, and an exact-arithmetic check on 200 random finite games agrees. The web appendix's extension to compact state spaces uses the convex hull without closure, which contains only finite mixtures of beliefs: with a state uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1] and a receiver who guesses only when certain, every finite signal yields 0 for the sender while full revelation yields 1, and the fix is the closed convex hull, under which existence survives. The rule of thumb that persuasion cannot help when the receiver's action and the sender's payoff are both concave fails because composing concave functions preserves concavity only if the outer function is increasing: with action p\sqrt{p}p​ and sender payoff −a-a−a, full disclosure beats silence at p=1/4p = 1/4p=1/4 (−1/4-1/4−1/4 against −1/2-1/2−1/2). In the lobbying application any signal that keeps every posterior mean at the prior mean is optimal for α<1/2\alpha < 1/2α<1/2, a worked example's bound becomes 1/21/21/2 against a true optimum of 1/31/31/3 once negative actions are ruled out, and Proposition 2 needs the stronger assumption that every action is uniquely optimal at some belief, which the web appendix already states. None of the errors affects the main theorems, but the rule of thumb is the kind of shortcut applied work reuses, and the appendix formula is cited as the reference result for continuous states.
