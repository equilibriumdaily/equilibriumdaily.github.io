The Hotelling Rule Holds Only on Average

Hotelling's rule says that the scarcity rent on an exhaustible resource should grow at the rate of interest. Over 65 years, the real prices of oil, metals and other exhaustible resources have risen relative to farm commodities by about what the rule implies at the average real interest rate. Over shorter periods the relation runs the wrong way: exhaustible prices fell fastest when real rates were highest, in the 1980s, and rose fastest when rates were low, in the 2000s.

Result

Over 1960-2024 the average trend of log real prices is 0.47 percent a year for 13 exhaustible resources and −1.69-1.69−1.69 percent for 10 renewable commodities, a gap of 2.16 points (s.e. 0.35); with the real ten-year Treasury yield of 1.94 percent, this implies a rent share of about 0.6 of the price. Across 46 rolling 20-year windows the exhaustible trend has a correlation of −0.68-0.68−0.68 with the real yield and the gap over renewables −0.02-0.02−0.02, and in annual data the response of exhaustible price changes to the lagged real yield is 0.21 (s.e. 0.83) against a predicted one.

Summary

If a mine owner can extract now or later, the price net of extraction cost must rise at the rate of interest. Because extraction and farm production share technological progress, the note compares World Bank real prices of 13 exhaustible resources with those of 10 renewable commodities, deflated by the US CPI, and relates the gap to real Treasury yields. With a rent share s of the price, the gap should be s times the real rate less the common cost trend. Over the full sample the gap of 2.16 points implies a share of about 0.6, and it is not driven by oil and gold: base metals alone show a gap of 1.36 points. Decade by decade the relation fails. Exhaustible prices fell 4.6 percent a year in 1980-99, when real yields averaged 4.1 percent, and rose 2.0 percent a year in 2000-24, when yields averaged 0.7 percent; renewable trends move the same way, with a correlation of −0.79-0.79−0.79 with the real yield across rolling windows. The common movement fits the cost of carry and demand, as in Frankel (2008), and the average gap is consistent with a Hotelling rent without identifying one.

    The Cost Disease Costs a Tenth of a Point

    Baumol's cost disease says that services with slow productivity growth, such as health care and education, become relatively more expensive, and take a growing share of spending if demand for them is price-inelastic. In US consumer spending since 1960 they have: health care and education rose from 6 to 20 percent of the budget as their relative price nearly tripled. The drag on measured productivity growth from this shift is small, about a tenth of a percentage point a year.

    Burgernomics After 25 Years: The Gap Closes Through Burger Prices

    The Economist's Big Mac index calls a currency overvalued when a burger costs more there in dollars than in the United States, which suggests the currency should fall. Over 25 years of the index, overvalued currencies have been no more likely to depreciate than any other. The burger gaps that do close, close because local menu prices catch up with past currency moves. National price levels under floating rates adjust through the exchange rate instead; only under the classical gold standard did price levels do the closing, as Big Mac prices do today.
