No-Arbitrage Does Not Imply an Equivalent Calibrated Pricing Measure

When a stock trades over two dates and options on its final price are priced by the market, one usually expects that ruling out arbitrage guarantees a pricing measure that agrees with the physical probabilities on which events are possible, prices every option correctly and makes the stock a fair game. In a simple, well-behaved market this fails: there is no arbitrage, yet every pricing measure that fits the option prices ignores a state that has physical probability one half.

Summary

Nutz, Wiesel and Zhao (2023) assume a calibrated martingale measure equivalent to the physical law with finite relative entropy and leave its characterization in terms of trading strategies as an open problem. The note shows that ordinary no-arbitrage is not that characterization. The counterexample links infinitely many two-point martingale rows in a single chain with weights tn=2−n/(nlog⁡2)t_n = 2^{-n}/(n \log 2)tn​=2−n/(nlog2), plus one extra row of physical probability one half. Calibrating to the terminal law propagates any weight on the extra row along the chain with alternating sign, so some even row would need negative probability; an arbitrage exploiting this would need an option payoff that doubles at each step down the chain, which is not integrable. The construction extends the non-closedness of semistatic outcomes found by Acciaio, Larsson and Schachermayer (2017) to a regular setting with a fixed physical law. With a closure condition the characterization returns through known results: by the Kreps-Yan theorem an equivalent calibrated measure exists if and only if the weak-star closure of the superhedging cone contains no free lunch, and a finite-entropy one exists if and only if no sequence of trades drives the expected exponential shortfall of a loss on any event down to its no-loss value.
