# Zipf's Law for US Cities, 2000-2024

## Intuition

Zipf's law, under which a city's size is inversely proportional to its rank, is often treated as a constant of urban economics. In US data for 2000-2024 it holds only for metropolitan areas at particular cut-offs; incorporated cities follow a clean power law, but with an exponent clearly above 1.

## Result

In US data for 2000-2024, Zipf's law with $\zeta = 1$ fails for both common city definitions: for metropolitan areas the exponent for the largest 135 is about 1.1 and not statistically different from 1, but the rank-size relation is curved, so a single power law is rejected; for incorporated cities the relation is straight, but $\zeta \approx 1.45$ is statistically above 1.

## Summary

Zipf's law says that the number of cities larger than $S$ is proportional to $S^{-\zeta}$ with $\zeta = 1$, the value implied by city growth unrelated to size (Gabaix 1999). Using Census Bureau population files for metropolitan statistical areas (2010, 2020, 2024) and incorporated places (2000, 2010, 2020, 2024), the note estimates $\zeta$ by the rank minus one half regression of Gabaix and Ibragimov (2011) and applies their curvature test. For metropolitan areas the curvature statistic is $-2.3$ to $-2.7$ for the largest 135 and $-3.0$ for the largest 250, and the estimate falls from 1.42 for the top 50 to 0.90 for all metros, so $\zeta$ is within 0.05 of 1 only for cut-offs between about 190 and 320. For cities proper the curvature statistic is near zero, while $\zeta$ is 1.41 to 1.46 for the largest 135 ($t$ of 2.4 to 2.6 against 1). Both patterns are stable from 2000 to 2024, so an exponent of 1 appears only for metropolitan areas at particular cut-offs.
