  • No. 002721 November 2026Sports Economics
    Participation Explains Half the Gender Gap at the Top of Chess

    Do the best men outrate the best women at chess simply because there are so many more men at the board? Reshuffling the sex labels of every active FIDE-rated player, as if each one were assigned by a coin heavily weighted towards men, reproduces about half of the gap between the world's 100 best men and 100 best women. At the very top, best man against best woman, the reshuffle explains only about a quarter, and further down the list women still average 115 points below men. Whatever explains the other half, this test cannot see it, so it offers a draw by agreement.

  • No. 002620 November 2026Macroeconomics
    When Interest Rates Ignored Inflation

    Under the gold standard, British long-term interest rates moved with the level of prices rather than with inflation, the regularity Keynes called Gibson's paradox. In two and a half centuries of Bank of England data the paradox appears whenever the pound was tied to gold and disappears under paper money, both during the Napoleonic suspension of convertibility and since 1973, when yields follow inflation instead. This is what Barsky and Summers (1988) predict if a fixed gold price makes the price level move with gold's real value.

  • No. 002519 November 2026Macroeconomics
    The Cost Disease Costs a Tenth of a Point

    Baumol's cost disease says that services with slow productivity growth, such as health care and education, become relatively more expensive, and take a growing share of spending if demand for them is price-inelastic. In US consumer spending since 1960 they have: health care and education rose from 6 to 20 percent of the budget as their relative price nearly tripled. The drag on measured productivity growth from this shift is small, about a tenth of a percentage point a year.

  • No. 002418 November 2026Macroeconomics
    The Hotelling Rule Holds Only on Average

    Hotelling's rule says that the scarcity rent on an exhaustible resource should grow at the rate of interest. Over 65 years, the real prices of oil, metals and other exhaustible resources have risen relative to farm commodities by about what the rule implies at the average real interest rate. Over shorter periods the relation runs the wrong way: exhaustible prices fell fastest when real rates were highest, in the 1980s, and rose fastest when rates were low, in the 2000s.

  • No. 002317 November 2026Industrial Organization
    Rockets and Feathers in the Egg Aisle

    Before 2015, retail egg prices followed wholesale prices up and down at the same speed. Since the bird-flu outbreaks began in 2015, they rise with wholesale costs much faster than they fall. In October 2026 wholesale eggs cost a third less than in 2019, yet a dozen in the shop cost two thirds more, far above what wholesale prices and general inflation imply.

  • No. 002216 November 2026Macroeconomics
    Burgernomics After 25 Years: The Gap Closes Through Burger Prices

    The Economist's Big Mac index calls a currency overvalued when a burger costs more there in dollars than in the United States, which suggests the currency should fall. Over 25 years of the index, overvalued currencies have been no more likely to depreciate than any other. The burger gaps that do close, close because local menu prices catch up with past currency moves. National price levels under floating rates adjust through the exchange rate instead; only under the classical gold standard did price levels do the closing, as Big Mac prices do today.

  • No. 002115 November 2026Asset Pricing
    The New Fama Puzzle Lives in Risk-Off Quarters

    Fama (1984) found that high-interest currencies tend to appreciate against the dollar, the opposite of what uncovered interest parity predicts. Since 2008 the sign has flipped, the new Fama puzzle. For ten currencies through 2025, the flip comes almost entirely from the few quarters in which risk rose sharply: then high-interest currencies fell against the dollar by far more than their rate differential. In all other quarters the slope is close to zero before and after 2008.

  • No. 002014 November 2026Market Microstructure
    One More Trader Always Helps in Nested Symmetric Markets: A Note on Rostek and Weretka (2012)

    Rostek and Weretka (2012) show that in small markets a larger market can have a less informative price, because entry can lower commonality, the average correlation between traders' values. Within their symmetric framework, a single trader added to a market without changing the incumbents' correlations cannot lower commonality, so the price becomes more informative with every entrant. Information dilution needs traders to arrive in groups or incumbents' correlations to change.

  • No. 001913 November 2026Mechanism Design
    The Bandit-Auction Index of Pavan, Segal and Toikka, Taken Literally

    Pavan, Segal and Toikka (2014) show how to sell to buyers who learn their values by consuming, allocating each period to the buyer with the highest virtual index. Their formula takes the expectation of a ratio of discounted sums, while the index that makes such rules optimal is a ratio of expectations. Taken literally, the printed formula undervalues experimentation and refuses trials that the optimal mechanism makes; the general theorems of the paper are unaffected.

  • No. 001812 November 2026Information Economics
    Publish the Rest: Research Funded by Trading Rents in a Kyle-Back Market

    Much research is paid for by the trading profits it makes possible, but keeping it private drives liquidity traders away. In a Kyle-Back market with endogenous participation, if all output must stay private the best signal is coarse, and every Gaussian private signal can be worse than no research. If part of the output can be published, the optimum keeps private only a small Gaussian component whose trading rent exactly covers the research bill and publishes the rest.
